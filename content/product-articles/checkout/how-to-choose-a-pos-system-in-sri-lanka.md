# How to Choose a POS System in Sri Lanka

The right point-of-sale system is not the one with the longest feature list. It is the one your staff can use at the counter, your stock records can rely on, and your business can still operate when conditions are less than ideal.

For a Sri Lankan retailer, that usually means evaluating more than billing speed. Internet availability, barcode hardware, receipt printing, purchasing, customer credit, staff access, branch visibility, data migration and local support can all affect whether the system survives daily use.

This guide provides a practical selection process for product-based retailers. Restaurants, appointment businesses and service companies have different workflows and should use requirements specific to their industries.

## Document a real sale before watching a demo

Write down how a normal sale works in your shop today:

1. How does the cashier find an item?
2. Are products scanned, searched or selected from a short list?
3. Do products have size, colour or other variants?
4. Which discounts can staff apply?
5. Do customers pay by cash, card, transfer, credit or a combination?
6. Can a sale be held and resumed?
7. What happens during a return or exchange?
8. When should stock reduce or be restored?
9. What appears on the receipt?
10. What must the manager review at closing?

Use this scenario during every vendor demonstration. A polished dashboard is less important than seeing your real checkout and exception cases completed correctly.

## Decide what must work without the internet

“Offline POS” can mean several different things. Ask the vendor to demonstrate exactly what remains available after disconnecting the device from the internet.

Check whether staff can still:

- Start and complete a sale
- Search products or scan barcodes
- View local stock
- Find a customer
- Hold and resume a cart
- Print a receipt
- Record cash and card payments
- Process an authorized return

Then ask what happens when connectivity returns. Which records synchronize, how are conflicts handled, and what does the cashier see if synchronization fails?

Some services will still require a connection even in an offline-first setup. Activation, cloud backup, online payment authorization, remote reporting and software updates are common examples. Get the boundary in writing.

## Match inventory control to the way stock moves

Basic stock deduction is not enough for many retailers. Map every way inventory enters, moves and leaves the business:

- Supplier purchases and goods received
- Sales and returns
- Damaged, expired or missing items
- Manual adjustments
- Transfers between counters or branches
- Items reserved in held orders
- Variants such as size and colour

Each adjustment should have a reason and history. If employees can change quantities without leaving a trace, the system will not help you understand why physical stock differs from the number on screen.

Ask how the POS handles stock counts, low-stock alerts, purchase returns and inventory valuation. If expiry dates matter to your products, include that in the demonstration rather than assuming it is standard.

## Test your actual barcodes and hardware

Hardware compatibility should be confirmed with exact models, ports and operating-system versions.

Bring sample products and test:

- Existing manufacturer barcodes
- Your own printed barcode labels
- Similar or damaged labels
- Products with multiple variants
- The receipt width and layout you intend to use
- Reprinting and cancelling a receipt

Ask whether the scanner behaves as a standard keyboard device or requires a driver. Confirm the supported receipt-printer protocol, paper width, connection type and cash-drawer trigger. A generic promise that the software “supports barcode scanners and printers” is not a compatibility list.

Checkout currently states that it supports selected barcode scanners and ESC/POS receipt printers on supported macOS setups. The exact hardware should be confirmed during quotation and testing; mobile and web applications are not currently presented as standard available products.

## Check payment and credit workflows

The POS should reflect how customers actually pay. Test:

- Full cash or card payment
- Split payment
- Partial payment
- Customer credit
- Payment recorded against an outstanding balance
- Refunds and returns
- Cancelled or held sales

Also distinguish between recording a card payment and processing it. A POS may record that a customer paid by card without integrating directly with the bank’s card terminal.

If your business offers customer credit, verify permissions, balance history, repayments and reporting. Staff should not be able to change balances without an audit trail.

## Evaluate purchasing, suppliers and returns

Inventory accuracy begins before the sale. Ask whether the system supports:

- Supplier records
- Purchase orders or purchase entries
- Partial deliveries
- Cost changes
- Supplier payments
- Purchase returns
- Stock updates linked to received goods

Use one real supplier invoice during the demo. Check how taxes, discounts, extra costs and returned items affect stock and reporting.

## Define staff access before creating accounts

List what cashiers, supervisors, inventory staff and owners should be allowed to do.

Sensitive actions commonly include:

- Applying large discounts
- Cancelling completed sales
- Processing returns
- Changing prices
- Adjusting stock
- Viewing profit or cost information
- Exporting customer data
- Reopening a closed shift

The system should identify the employee responsible for important changes. Shared administrator logins remove that accountability.

## Test branch and till behavior

If you operate—or expect to operate—more than one branch, clarify whether each location has separate stock and how owners see the combined business.

Ask:

- Is stock maintained by branch, warehouse and till?
- How are transfers requested, sent and received?
- Can one branch see another branch’s availability?
- What happens when two branches are offline?
- Are prices and products shared centrally?
- Can permissions differ by branch?
- Are consolidated reports available?

Do not pay for a complex multi-branch system if you operate one simple counter. But do not choose a system that requires a complete replacement as soon as the second location opens.

## Review reporting through decisions, not chart count

Ask the vendor to answer real management questions:

- Which products are selling and which are not moving?
- What is currently low in stock?
- What was the day’s sales total by payment method?
- Which returns or cancellations occurred, and who approved them?
- What do customers owe?
- What do we owe suppliers?
- What is the current inventory value?
- Can the accountant receive a useful export?

A report is valuable when someone can act on it. More charts do not necessarily mean better control.

## Understand backup, ownership and exit

Ask where the data is stored, how it is backed up and how recovery is tested.

You should also know:

- Who owns the business data?
- Which export formats are available?
- Can products, customers, sales and stock history be exported?
- What happens when a subscription ends?
- How is an offline device recovered after hardware failure?
- Who can access backups?

Do not treat “cloud backup included” as a complete recovery plan. Ask the provider to explain the restoration process and expected recovery time.

## Calculate the total cost of the working setup

Compare costs over a realistic period, not only the advertised monthly or lifetime price.

Include:

- Software licence or subscription
- Additional branches, tills and staff accounts
- Computer or supported terminal
- Barcode scanners
- Receipt printers and cash drawers
- Setup and data migration
- Training
- Custom reports or integrations
- Support and on-site visits
- Updates and backup services
- Replacement hardware

A lower entry price can be appropriate for a simple shop. A more expensive system can be better value if it removes manual reconciliation or supports a workflow you would otherwise build around it. Make the comparison using the same requirements.

## Run a pilot with acceptance criteria

Before full rollout, test with representative products, staff and transactions.

A useful pilot checklist includes:

- Scan and sell common products
- Process every accepted payment type
- Hold, resume, cancel and return a sale
- Receive a supplier purchase
- Adjust stock with a reason
- Print and reprint receipts
- Disconnect the internet and complete the agreed offline workflow
- Reconnect and confirm synchronization
- Run closing reports
- Restore a backup in a controlled test

Record pass, fail and follow-up items. Verbal assurances should not replace acceptance testing.

## A concise POS selection checklist

Choose a system only after you can answer yes to the questions that matter for your operation:

- Does it support our type of retail?
- Can staff complete our normal and exceptional sales?
- Does the required work continue without internet?
- Are our exact hardware models supported?
- Does stock move through the system as it moves physically?
- Are staff actions permissioned and recorded?
- Can it support our current and near-term branch structure?
- Are reports useful to the owner and accountant?
- Is migration, training and support clearly scoped?
- Can we retrieve our data if we leave?
- Do we understand the complete cost?
