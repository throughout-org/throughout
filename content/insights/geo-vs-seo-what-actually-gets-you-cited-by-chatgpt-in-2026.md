SEO, answer engine optimization (AEO) and generative engine optimization (GEO) are often described as three separate disciplines. That framing is useful for discussing different search experiences, but it can also encourage teams to invent an “AI strategy” that ignores the foundations already required for search.

Google’s own guidance is direct: foundational SEO practices remain relevant to its generative search features, and there is no special schema type or secret technical requirement for appearing in AI Overviews or AI Mode. OpenAI similarly explains that a site must allow its search crawler if it wants to be eligible for inclusion in ChatGPT search results.

So what actually changes?

## SEO is the foundation

SEO ensures that search systems can access, understand and evaluate a page, and that the page satisfies a real search need.

The work includes:

- Crawlability and indexability
- Clear site architecture and internal links
- Useful, original content
- Page experience and performance
- Accurate titles and descriptions
- Relevant links and reputation signals
- Structured data that matches visible content
- Measurement through search and analytics data

These are not “old search” concerns. Google’s AI features draw on pages from its search index. A page that cannot be crawled, indexed or understood does not gain an advantage merely because the result is generated by AI.

## AEO focuses on direct answers

AEO is commonly used for work that helps a system answer a question directly. Historically that included featured snippets and voice assistants. Today it also overlaps with generative answers.

Useful AEO practices are mostly good information design:

- Use descriptive headings.
- Answer the question before adding secondary context.
- Define terms precisely.
- Make comparisons explicit.
- Keep facts close to their supporting sources.
- Use tables or steps when they genuinely improve comprehension.

This does not mean every heading must be a question or every paragraph must be reduced to a tiny “AI-friendly chunk.” Google explicitly says there is no ideal page length or requirement to rewrite content into fragments for its AI experiences.

## GEO focuses on selection inside generated answers

GEO describes the attempt to improve how a brand, source or piece of content appears in generative answers.

Unlike a conventional results page, an AI answer can combine several retrieved sources. Systems may issue related searches—often called query fan-out—to gather material about different parts of a complex question. The resulting answer may cite several pages, cite none, or vary between otherwise similar prompts.

That changes the unit of measurement. Instead of watching only one keyword position, a team may need to observe:

- Whether the brand is mentioned for a defined set of prompts
- Which pages or third-party sources are cited
- How accurately the brand is described
- Whether citations lead to visits
- Whether those visits produce useful outcomes
- How results change by engine, location, account state and time

AI visibility is probabilistic. One manual prompt is not a reliable benchmark.

## What does not have good evidence behind it

AI-search advice often moves faster than the available evidence. Treat the following claims cautiously:

- A guaranteed formula for earning ChatGPT citations
- A universal word count for AI visibility
- A special schema type that makes a page “GEO ready”
- A promise that adding an `llms.txt` file will improve Google visibility
- A fixed relationship between a Google ranking and an AI citation
- Guaranteed placement based on buying mentions or publishing at scale

Google says it ignores `llms.txt` for ranking and visibility in Google Search. It also warns against commodity content and inauthentic mentions. OpenAI notes that search citations can be incomplete, outdated or incorrect, which is another reason to measure patterns rather than celebrate one answer.

## What is worth doing

### Publish information worth retrieving

Generic summaries compete with thousands of similar pages. Original experience, tested workflows, first-party data, clear product documentation and expert analysis give a retrieval system a reason to select your page.

### Make the page accessible

Allow the relevant crawler, return a successful response, expose important content as text, and link to it internally. Check CDN and security rules as well as `robots.txt`.

### State facts clearly and support them

Name the source, link to primary documentation, show the method behind original research, and display a meaningful update date. Do not manufacture statistics to make a paragraph look authoritative.

### Keep entity information consistent

Names, product descriptions, locations and ownership details should agree across the website and maintained public profiles. Structured data can reinforce accurate visible information, but it should not make claims the page itself does not support.

### Measure a prompt set over time

Create a stable set of prompts based on real buyer questions. Record the engine, date, location assumptions, response, citations and resulting referral traffic. Re-run the same set at a sensible interval.

## SEO and GEO should share one workflow

The most sustainable approach is not two disconnected content programs. Research the user’s problem once, create the best source you can, ensure it is technically accessible, distribute it where the audience already looks, and measure both search performance and AI-assisted discovery.

SEO tells you whether your pages are discoverable and competitive in search. AI-visibility monitoring adds another view of how those pages and your wider reputation are represented in generated answers.

The labels may continue to evolve. The core responsibility does not: publish accurate, useful information that search systems can access and people can trust.
