“Should we automate this or hire someone?” sounds like a cost comparison. Usually it is a workflow-design question first.

A new employee is not interchangeable with software. A person can interpret ambiguity, build relationships, notice unusual conditions and take responsibility. Automation can repeat a defined process consistently and handle volume without copying information between systems. Comparing an annual salary with a monthly software subscription ignores most of the real cost and nearly all of the risk.

Use this framework to decide which investment fits the work.

## Describe the workload before comparing solutions

Choose one workflow, not an entire job title. Document:

- What starts the work?
- Which inputs are required?
- What decisions are made?
- Which systems are updated?
- What exceptions occur?
- Who approves the outcome?
- How often does the work happen?
- What happens when it is wrong or late?

“Customer service” is too broad. “Classify an incoming enquiry, retrieve an order status and draft a reply for approval” is specific enough to evaluate.

## Identify the kind of work involved

### Repeatable processing

Examples include moving validated data, generating a standard report, sending a scheduled reminder or routing a request using known rules. This is the strongest automation candidate.

### Assisted judgement

The system can retrieve information or prepare a recommendation, but a person evaluates it. Examples include drafting a response, flagging an unusual transaction or summarizing a contract for review.

### Accountable human judgement

Negotiation, sensitive employee decisions, high-risk approvals, complex customer recovery and strategy usually require a responsible person. Software may support the work but should not own the decision.

Many useful systems combine all three rather than trying to remove people entirely.

## Calculate the current process cost

Measure the existing workflow for a representative period:

- Number of cases
- Average handling time
- Rework and correction time
- Waiting time between handoffs
- Cost of missed or late cases
- Manager review time
- Software already used

Use actual internal compensation and overhead data if comparing with hiring. Public salary averages may not reflect the role, country, seniority or employment model you need.

The current cost is not only labour. A slow process can delay revenue or create customer churn, but those effects should be measured rather than guessed.

## Calculate the full automation cost

The subscription is often the smallest line item. Include:

- Process discovery and design
- Integration or custom development
- Data cleanup
- Vendor and model usage fees
- Security and privacy review
- Testing and monitoring
- Human approval time
- Exception handling
- Maintenance when APIs or processes change
- Training and operational documentation
- The cost of incorrect output

An inexpensive tool that requires daily correction may cost more than it saves.

## Calculate the full hiring cost

Use your organization’s real figures for:

- Compensation and statutory obligations
- Recruitment
- Equipment and software
- Onboarding and training
- Management time
- Time until the person is effective
- Leave and coverage planning

Then include the value the person may add beyond the narrow workflow: customer knowledge, process improvement, flexibility and accountability. A simplistic spreadsheet often counts these as zero.

## Compare capacity, not only price

Estimate the number of correctly completed cases each option can support.

For automation:

`usable capacity = processed cases − failed cases − cases requiring rework`

For hiring:

`usable capacity = available work time − meetings − administration − training − rework`

The formulas are intentionally plain. The quality of the inputs matters more than a sophisticated ROI percentage.

## Price the exceptions

Automation looks strongest when only the happy path is modelled. Review the last 50 or 100 cases and classify the exceptions:

- Missing information
- Conflicting data
- Customer changes
- Policy exceptions
- System outages
- Suspicious or unsafe requests
- Cases requiring empathy or negotiation

Decide which exceptions can be handled with rules, which need human review and which should stop the workflow entirely.

If exceptions are frequent and expensive, improving the process may be more valuable than automating it.

## Run a bounded pilot

Choose one measurable segment. Keep the existing fallback available and define success before starting.

Measure:

- Completion rate
- Accuracy
- Human review time
- Exception rate
- Time saved
- User or customer outcome
- Total operating cost
- Incidents and near misses

Do not scale because a demo looked impressive. Scale because the pilot produced reliable outcomes under realistic conditions.

## When automation is the better next step

Automation is promising when:

- Inputs are available digitally.
- The process is frequent and reasonably stable.
- Rules or review criteria can be documented.
- Errors are detectable and recoverable.
- Systems provide reliable integration points.
- A person remains accountable for exceptions.

## When hiring is the better next step

Hiring is usually stronger when:

- The work depends on trust and relationships.
- The process changes constantly.
- The organization needs someone to own and improve the process.
- Exceptions are the main work rather than the edge case.
- Wrong decisions carry material human, legal or financial consequences.
- The business lacks enough process clarity to automate responsibly.

## Consider a hybrid design

A common answer is to hire or retain the person who owns the outcome and automate the repetitive preparation around them.

For example, software can collect the required information, identify missing fields, retrieve account history and draft a response. A team member reviews the context, makes the decision and communicates with the customer.

This can increase capacity without pretending the human contribution is merely a cost to eliminate.

## Make the decision reversible

Avoid designs that trap the business in one vendor or remove the ability to operate manually. Keep export paths, document the workflow, retain audit logs and establish a fallback for outages.

The best first automation is usually a bounded process with clear evidence, recoverable errors and a measurable owner—not a plan to replace an entire role.
