Choose a project team when you can describe the outcome and want it delivered against a scope and timeline. Choose a dedicated team when the work is continuous, the scope will keep changing, and you want engineers who accumulate knowledge of your product over time.

Most startups start with a project and move to a dedicated team once the product has users. Here is how the two models differ, and how to decide.

## What is a project team?

A project team is assembled to deliver a defined result, such as a marketing site, an MVP or a mobile app. Scope, timeline and price are agreed up front, and the studio manages delivery. You review progress and accept the work.

## What is a dedicated development team?

A dedicated team is a group of developers, designers, QA or DevOps engineers who work only on your product, inside your tools, standups and roadmap. You set priorities. The studio handles hiring, payroll, equipment and continuity. Cost is usually a recurring monthly amount tied to team size.

## How do the two models compare?

The two models differ mainly in how scope, cost and control work. A project team is paid for a defined outcome and managed by the studio, while a dedicated team is paid for capacity and directed by you. The table below sets out the practical differences.

| | Project team | Dedicated team |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | A defined website, app or MVP | Ongoing product development |
| Scope | Fixed or tightly bounded | Evolves with your roadmap |
| Who sets priorities | Agreed in the scope | You, continuously |
| Cost behaviour | Price tied to scope | Monthly cost tied to team size |
| Management effort on your side | Lower | Higher |
| Change handling | Through change requests | Reprioritise the backlog |
| Knowledge retention | Handover at the end | Builds up in the team |
| Main risk | Scope disagreements | Under-using the team |

## When is a project team the right choice?

A project team is the right choice when the outcome is definable, the budget is fixed and you cannot give daily direction. If you can write acceptance criteria and the work has a natural end such as a launch, a defined scope gives both sides clarity. Use these tests:

- You can write acceptance criteria for the outcome.
- You want a fixed budget conversation before you start.
- You do not have a product owner with daily availability.
- The work has a natural end, such as a launch.

## When is a dedicated team the right choice?

A dedicated team is the right choice when the roadmap is long, the scope will change as you learn and you can supply a product owner. It works best for products with users or funding, where retained knowledge and steady capacity matter more than a fixed price. Use these tests:

- The roadmap is long and will change as you learn.
- You already have users, revenue or a funded plan.
- You can supply a product owner who prioritises weekly or daily.
- You are weighing hiring locally and want capacity faster, without recruitment overhead.

## Can you combine them?

Yes, and it is common. Run the first release as a project so scope and price are explicit. Once the product is live and the backlog grows, keep the people who built it as a dedicated team. ## Common mistakes

Most problems come from picking the model before understanding the work. The costliest mistakes are paying for capacity without a backlog, fixing scope for a product you do not understand yet, lacking a single decision-maker and comparing only the monthly number. Each has a simple prevention:

- **Buying a dedicated team without a backlog.** Idle capacity is the most expensive outcome.
- **Fixing scope for a product you do not understand yet.** Discovery work first, build second.
- **No single decision-maker.** Both models stall without one.
- **Comparing only the monthly number.** Include management time, rework and continuity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a dedicated team more expensive than a project team?

Not necessarily. A project price covers a scope, while a dedicated team cost covers capacity over time. Over a long roadmap, a dedicated team can be cheaper per unit of work because context is retained. For a short, bounded build, a project is usually simpler.

### How big should a dedicated team be?

Start small, often two to four people, and grow when the backlog is consistently larger than capacity. A typical early team might include a developer or two, a designer and part-time QA.

### Can I scale a dedicated team up or down?

Usually yes, with notice agreed in the contract. Ask about the notice period and minimum term before you sign.

### Do I own the work in either model?

You should in both. Confirm ownership of code, designs and content in the contract.
